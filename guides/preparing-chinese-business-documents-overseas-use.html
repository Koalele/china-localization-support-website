Preparing Chinese Business Documents for Overseas Use

A practical checklist for preparing Chinese documents that overseas customers, partners and institutions can understand and use.

24 November 2026 · 4 min read · TransWind

When a Chinese document has to travel across borders, the translation itself is only one part of the job. The receiving party needs a clear, complete record that connects to the original document. Whether you are sending company materials to an overseas partner or preparing documents for an application, this checklist helps you avoid the delays caused by missing pages, inconsistent names and unclear scans.

1. Start with the receiving party’s requirements

Ask the recipient what they need before translation begins. Some organisations accept a digital copy; others ask for a signed translation, a certification statement or an original document to be shown alongside the translation. Their requirements decide the format, timing and supporting paperwork.

Confirm four points in writing: the required language, whether certification is needed, whether every stamp and handwritten note must be translated, and the deadline.

2. Send complete, readable source files

Provide the full document, including covers, appendices, seals, signatures and reverse sides. A sharp scan or straight-on photo is better than a compressed screenshot. If a page is faint, cropped or partly covered, send another image before the translator starts.

For multi-page documents, keep the pages in order and give files simple names. If you have several versions of the same document, label the current one clearly so an outdated draft is not translated by mistake.

3. Check names and numbers first

The most costly errors in business documents are often small ones. Before work begins, provide the preferred English spelling of company names, personal names, addresses and product names. Match them to passports, company registrations, contracts or previous approved translations where relevant.

  • Company names and registration numbers
  • Names of directors, signatories and contacts
  • Addresses, dates and currencies
  • Product names, model numbers and technical terms

Tell the translator if a word should remain in Chinese, for example a brand name or a legal title that has an established English form.

4. Keep the source and translation together

A translated document should be easy to compare with its Chinese source. Keep the original files, the final translation and any certification statement in one folder. Use a consistent filename so colleagues and recipients can identify the document without opening several versions.

If the document will be reviewed by people in different countries, a bilingual file or a short cover note can make the handover clearer. The note should explain what the document is, who issued it and which version is attached.

5. Review before sending

Ask the person who knows the document best to check the final version. They should confirm that names, amounts, dates, page numbers and attachments are complete. This is also the moment to check that every seal, signature and important heading appears in the translation.

Do not wait until the recipient raises a question. A five-minute review before sending is much easier than correcting a submitted document under a deadline.

6. Allow time for questions and updates

Documents often change after the first translation: a revised contract, a new bank detail or a request for another supporting page. Keep an editable source version where possible and tell the translator exactly what changed. Sending only the revised page usually makes updates faster and reduces the chance of replacing the wrong text.
