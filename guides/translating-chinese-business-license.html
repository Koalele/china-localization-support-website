Translating Your Chinese Business License and Company Documents

A practical guide for overseas companies working with Chinese suppliers, partners or subsidiaries.

18 November 2026 · 4 min read · TransWind

If you buy from a Chinese supplier, set up a joint venture or open an office in China, sooner or later someone will ask for an English version of a Chinese company document. Your bank needs it for checks, your auditor for the file, a lawyer or a government office for a filing. Here is what usually needs translating, what is on a Chinese business license, and how to make sure the translation is accepted the first time.

1. Documents that usually need translating

  • Business license (营业执照): the basic proof that the company exists.
  • Articles of association (公司章程): who owns the company and how it is run.
  • Financial statements and audit reports: for due diligence, banks and investors.
  • Contracts and powers of attorney: anything someone will sign or rely on.
  • Licenses and certificates for specific products or industries.

2. What is on a Chinese business license

Every business license follows the same layout. The main fields are:

  • Unified social credit code: an 18-character ID. It is the most reliable way to identify the company.
  • Company name: the registered name is the Chinese one. An English name used on a website or invoice is usually not registered, so a good translation keeps the Chinese name next to the English.
  • Company type, legal representative, registered capital, date of establishment and address.
  • Business scope: what the company is allowed to do. It is often long, and it should be translated in full.
  • Registration authority and date, with the authority's seal and a QR code.

Before you rely on a license, you can look the company up by name or credit code on the official National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System.

3. Certified or standard translation?

For internal reading, such as checking a supplier, a standard translation is enough. For a bank, a court, an immigration office or a company registry abroad, you will usually need a certified translation: one that carries the translation company's seal and a signed statement that it is accurate and complete. Requirements differ between authorities and countries, so ask the receiving office what it accepts before you order.

4. Notarization and apostille

Some offices also want the original document notarized or authenticated. Since 7 November 2023 China has been part of the Apostille Convention, so a Chinese public document going to another member country can get an apostille instead of going through consular legalization. Apostilles are issued by China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs or by local foreign affairs offices it has authorized (Chinese Consulate-General in Manchester). Ask the receiving office whether the translation should be attached before or after this step.

5. Tips for a smooth job

  • Send a clear scan of every page, including seals and stamps.
  • Tell us what the translation is for and which country will receive it.
  • If you already have English versions of the company name or people's names, share them so they stay consistent across all documents.
  • Order all related documents together. It saves time and keeps the terms the same.

6. What it costs

Short documents such as a business license are usually priced as one certified document, from ¥150 into English, ¥180 into Japanese and ¥200 into French. Longer texts such as articles of association or audit reports are priced per 1,000 Chinese characters, from ¥220 into English. Legal and financial texts are priced according to difficulty.
