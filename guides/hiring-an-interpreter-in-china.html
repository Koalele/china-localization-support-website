Hiring an Interpreter in China: Costs, Types and What to Ask

How to choose the right interpreter for meetings, factory visits and trade shows in China, and what to ask before you book.

7 November 2026 · 4 min read · TransWind

A good interpreter can be the difference between a supplier meeting that ends in a clear deal and one that ends in polite confusion. If you are visiting China for meetings, factory tours or a trade show, here is how to choose the right kind of interpreter, what it usually costs, and what to ask before you book.

1. Three types of interpreting

Business escort (liaison) interpreting. One interpreter goes with you for the day: company visits, factory floors, trade show booths, business meals. They interpret short exchanges in both directions. This is what most business visitors need.

Consecutive interpreting. The speaker pauses every few sentences and the interpreter renders what was said, often from notes. It suits negotiations, technical discussions and formal meetings where accuracy matters more than speed.

Simultaneous interpreting. The interpreter speaks at the same time as the speaker, usually from a booth, with listeners wearing headsets. It needs equipment, and interpreters usually work in pairs and take turns, so it is mostly used for conferences (Omniglot: modes of interpreting).

2. What it costs in Wuhan

As a guide, our own starting rates for Chinese–English interpreting are:

  • Business escort interpreting: from ¥1,500 per day (up to 8 hours)
  • Consecutive interpreting: from ¥3,000 per day
  • Business visit packages with briefing and same-day notes: from ¥1,200 for a half day and ¥2,800 for a full day

Japanese and French usually cost a little more, and simultaneous interpreting is quoted per event because of the equipment and team. Whoever you hire, ask for the total in writing, including overtime, travel time and meals.

3. Questions to ask before you book

  • Has the interpreter worked in your industry, or with factories and suppliers?
  • Will you get the same person for the whole trip?
  • Is there a briefing call before the first meeting?
  • What is included: hours, travel time, meals, overtime?
  • Can they share a short written summary or notes after meetings?
  • What happens if a meeting runs late or the schedule changes?

4. How to get the most from your interpreter

  • Send product specifications, drawings and a list of key terms a few days ahead.
  • Speak in short sections and pause. Long monologues lose detail.
  • Talk to your counterpart, not to the interpreter.
  • Confirm numbers out loud: prices, quantities, dates.
  • Agree in advance whether the interpreter should tell you about cultural cues or tone they notice.

5. Don't rely on the supplier's interpreter

Many suppliers offer their own staff to interpret. It is convenient, but that person works for the other side. For anything involving prices, quality or contract terms, bring your own interpreter so you know exactly what is being said.
