Visiting Factories in Wuhan: A Practical Checklist for Overseas Buyers

A short checklist for buyers meeting suppliers in Wuhan and central China, from booking the trip to following up.

24 October 2026 · 4 min read · TransWind

Wuhan sits in the middle of China’s high-speed rail network and is home to large automotive, optoelectronics and manufacturing clusters. If you are planning to meet suppliers here, a little preparation turns a tiring trip into decisions you can act on.

1. Before you book

Check your entry rules. Citizens of France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Switzerland and more than 30 other countries can currently enter China without a visa for up to 30 days for business, tourism or exchange. The policy runs until 31 December 2026 (Beijing Municipal Government). Check the latest list with the Chinese embassy in your country before you travel.

Set up payments and your phone. Most payments in China are made with Alipay or WeChat Pay, and both accept many overseas bank cards (Shanghai Municipal Government). Install them before you fly, together with a translation app and a map app that works in China.

Plan the route, not just the dates. Factories around Wuhan can be an hour or more from the city centre. Group visits by district and allow realistic travel time between them. Two well-prepared meetings a day are better than four rushed ones.

2. Prepare the meeting, not just the trip

  • Send each supplier your product specifications, drawings and target quantities a week ahead.
  • Write down the five questions you must have answered: price at your quantity, minimum order quantity, lead time, payment terms and quality control.
  • Bring bilingual business cards. Chinese counterparts often exchange cards first and talk second.
  • Brief your interpreter on your product and the terms you will use. A 30-minute call before the visit saves an hour of confusion on the factory floor.

3. On the day

  • Ask to see the production line and the quality-control area, not only the showroom.
  • Confirm numbers out loud and ask your interpreter to read them back: prices, quantities, dates.
  • Take photos only where you are allowed, and note which photo belongs to which supplier.
  • Lunch with the supplier is common. It is part of building the relationship, so leave time for it.

4. The evening after

The most expensive mistake is not writing things down. Before you sleep, make a short note for each supplier: who you met, what they quoted, what they promised and what happens next. If you worked with an interpreter, ask for bilingual meeting notes the same evening.

5. Within three days

Send each supplier a short follow-up in Chinese and English that confirms what was agreed and lists the next steps. A clear written follow-up is often what separates suppliers who deliver from those who do not.
